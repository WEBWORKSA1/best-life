Relationships: the strongest predictor of a good life

What long-running research says about connection — and the small weekly habits that build it.

By the Best.Life editorial team · 3 min read · Reviewed 2026-11-19

Key takeaways

  • Close relationships predict long-term wellbeing better than income or status.
  • Connection is built from frequency, not grand gestures.
  • Listening well is the rarest and most valued social skill.

When researchers follow people across decades and ask what separated those who thrived from those who did not, the answer is consistent and a little inconvenient for anyone hoping for a clever hack: warm, dependable relationships. Not the number of friends, and not being in a couple — the presence of people you can count on, and who can count on you.

Frequency beats intensity

Friendships fade through neglect far more often than through conflict. Adults lose touch because nothing in their calendar makes contact automatic. Fix the structure and the feeling follows:

  • One recurring weekly slot — a walk, a call, a meal — with someone who matters.
  • One message a day to a person you have not spoken to in a month. Thirty seconds, no agenda.
  • One standing monthly gathering that happens whether or not everyone can come.

Be the one who listens

Most people in a conversation are waiting to speak. If you ask a real question, stay quiet for the answer and then ask one more, you will be remembered as unusually good company. Put the phone out of sight; its mere presence on the table lowers the quality of the exchange.

Say the appreciative thing out loud

We assume the people close to us know what we value about them. Usually they do not. Specific appreciation — "I noticed you stayed late to help me finish that, it meant a lot" — costs nothing and is remembered for years.

Repair quickly

Strong relationships are not the ones without friction; they are the ones where repair happens fast. A plain "I was short with you earlier, I am sorry" within the day prevents the slow build-up that actually ends relationships.

If you are starting from lonely

Loneliness is common and nothing to be ashamed of. The reliable route out is repeated, low-pressure contact around a shared activity: a class, a volunteer shift, a running group, a faith community. Go to the same thing at the same time for six weeks. Familiarity does the work that confidence cannot.
